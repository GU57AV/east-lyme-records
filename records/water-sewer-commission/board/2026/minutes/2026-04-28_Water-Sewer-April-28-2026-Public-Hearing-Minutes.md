2. Discussion on Proposed Water Rates

   Note Ben North, Chief Operating Officer of the Water Department, came forward and noted the publisher's
  certificate for the record.
         Publisher's_Certificate.pdf

   Note Mr. North explained he would discuss the Water Department side of the proposed rate increases and the
  budget process and stated the primary reason for the rate increases is the need to address PFAS
  contamination in the town’s water system.

  Note see attachment.
       Proposed_water_rates.pdf

  Note He explained the proposed rate structure changes which include a new irrigation sub‑metering option so
  irrigation users pay their fair share, a low‑usage tier for customers using under 10,000 gallons every six
  months to encourage conservation, a simpler billing structure to make bills easier to understand, and higher
  fees for manual meter reads to encourage automated readings and reduce costs.

  Note Mr. North explained that after the town completed a major water meter replacement project, the
  Department gathered more accurate data about water usage. They hey discovered that summer water
  demand is extremely high. It was originally believed that tourist activity and restaurants caused the increase,
  but new data shows that lawn irrigation systems are actually the biggest cause of excessive summer water
  use. Seasonal lawn watering creates major stress on the water system during the exact time when water
  supply is most limited.

  Note Mr. North said that several town well sites have stream flow restrictions- the town cannot pump unlimited
  water and during dry periods, stream levels drop too low. When that happens, DEEP restrictions limit how
  much water can be pumped; water is a limited resource and cannot simply be created.

   Note He said the Commission identified lawn irrigation as one of the biggest issues affecting the system and
  they wanted to reduce unnecessary water demand, encourage conservation, and avoid imposing mandatory
  restrictions if possible. Instead of immediately requiring irrigation controls, the Commission decided to create
  incentives to encourage reduced usage.

   Note He added that the existing rate structure treated many users similarly even when their water usage was
  drastically different. Some customers use very little water but still face the same basic charges, many residents
  are on fixed incomes, and they wanted to protect lower-use customers from major increases.
  The goal was to shield customers using less than 10,000 gallons during a six-month period from some of the
  financial impact.

   Note Mr. North further explained that the town must install PFAS treatment systems to comply with federal
  regulations by 2029. PFAS treatment projects are expensive, and these costs are part of the reason rates
  must increase.

   Note He explained that the proposed irrigation sub‑metering program would address the current problem of
  residents paying both water and sewer charges even when irrigation water never enters the sewer system.
  Under the plan, homeowners could install a separate irrigation meter, paying the upfront installation cost while
  the Water & Sewer Department would take over long‑term maintenance. Irrigation water would then be billed
  separately at a lower rate with no sewer fees applied. Mr. North emphasized that this is designed as an
  optional incentive program rather than a mandatory requirement.

  Note Mr. North reported that data from roughly 7,100 service connections shows a clear pattern of heavy
  seasonal irrigation use concentrated among a small group of households. He found that 300-500 accounts,
  mostly single‑family homes, appear to operate irrigation systems, with some using 80,000 to 400,000 gallons
  in a six‑month period.
  By comparison, a typical household uses only 20,000-30,000 gallons over the same timeframe. He
  emphasized that a very small percentage of customers drives a disproportionate share of demand- just 5% of
  residential users consume 27% of all summer water, totaling approximately 46 million gallons. East Lyme does
  not have an unlimited water supply, and the town must carefully manage water resources because supply is
  dependent on natural conditions such as rainfall and stream flow.

   Note He explained the purpose of the new rate structure was to create:
  *A more equitable billing system.
  *Incentives for conservation.
  *Better distribution of infrastructure costs.

   Note Mr. North added that roughly 95% of residential customers use less than the Department’s high‑volume
  threshold, while the remaining 5% of customers account for 27% of all summer water consumption, totaling
  about 46 million gallons. These high‑volume users are generally households using more than 40,000 gallons in
  a six‑month period-often substantially more. This concentrated demand places strain on infrastructure, drives
  higher operating costs, and increases the risk during drought or low‑stream‑flow conditions.

  Note The proposed rate changes are intended to:
  *Protect lower-volume users.
  *Encourage conservation.
  *Reduce excessive irrigation demand.
  *Help fund federally required PFAS treatment systems,
  *Make billing more proportional to actual usage.

   Note Mr. North emphasized that the proposed irrigation submeter program would be voluntary rather than
  mandatory. Under the proposal:
  *Homeowners choosing irrigation meters would pay installation costs.
  *The Water & Sewer Department would maintain the meters afterward.
  *Irrigation water would be billed separately.
  *Irrigation-only water would avoid sewer charges.
  He explained the Commission viewed this as a fairer approach for customers whose outdoor watering never
  enters the sewer system.

  Note Mr. North shared that seasonal water production nearly doubles in summer, rising from 40-50 million
  gallons per month in winter to roughly 80 million gallons per month in summer, at the exact time when stream
  flow is low, wells are stressed, reservoirs are depleted, and environmental pumping limits tighten.

   Note He reminded the Public that the town built a $10 million connection to New London to get extra water
  during peak demand, but the deal is very expensive because East Lyme must give back 15% of whatever it
  borrows for free and then buy water from New London at full retail price. This is not a realistic long‑term
  solution.
  He also stressed that about 300 households out of 7,100 use 27% of all summer water, which is a major
  reason the Commission is proposing changes to the rate structure. Master‑metered apartment buildings are
  also being unfairly pushed into higher tiers because one big meter makes their usage look excessive, even
  though the water is shared across many units. The Department plans to help these buildings rework their
  metering, so they are billed more fairly.

  Note The basic philosophy behind the new rates is that people who use less water should pay less, and very
  high‑volume users should pay more because their heavy use requires bigger tanks, pumps, pipes, wells, and
  overall system capacity. In simple terms, high‑volume users put more strain on the system, so they cost the
  utility more.

  Note Mr. North then addressed water quality concerns, explaining that the town discovered PFAS
  contamination at two major well sites a couple of years ago, and now only has a few years left before federal
  EPA deadlines take effect. The two PFAS treatment projects are expected to cost about $17 million and will
  require new treatment buildings, new filtration systems, and large carbon filter vessels, each roughly 14 feet
  wide and 30 feet tall. One site will need two vessels, and the other will need four, all designed to filter water
  after it is pumped and treated.

   Note He also reviewed existing treatment steps- chlorination, pH adjustment, fluoride, corrosion control, and
  iron and manganese removal, and reminded residents that older issues like brown or black sediment were
  caused by iron and manganese, which the town addressed through years of upgrades, most recently in 2023.
  Water systems face continually increasing federal and state requirements, including PFAS rules and lead and
  copper regulations, and treatment standards keep rising, so operating a safe water system is becoming more
  expensive.

  Note Mr. North outlined several other major capital needs the Department must plan for. The Roxbury Road
  water tank near the transfer station will need to be repainted within the next five years, while the tank is not in
  poor condition today, it still requires long‑term maintenance planning.
  He said the Department also needs to upgrade its booster stations and replace old tanks, some of which are
  40-50 years old and close to wearing out. The town must now invest much more in cybersecurity and building
  security because water systems are facing increasing threats including foreign‑linked attacks, hacking
  attempts on municipal systems, and other risks to water and sewer infrastructure. Water systems are now
  targeted much like airports and other major utilities, which means security upgrades are no longer optional but
  a necessary part of running a modern water system.

   Note Mr. North North said the town is looking at Plant’s Dam as its next major water‑supply project, since it's
  the last big aquifer left in East Lyme and one of the only ways to meaningfully expand capacity. He also
  shared that the town is now going through the last of the large rate increases, including the current proposed
  13% hike. He explained that the Commission began planning these increases three to four years ago, right
  after PFAS contamination was discovered, so the town could raise revenue slowly instead of hitting residents
  with sudden, massive jumps.
  The town cannot pay the full $17 million PFAS treatment cost upfront because the Water Department’s annual
  budget is only about $5 million, meaning the projects must be financed through borrowing. The current rate
  increases are meant to build enough revenue to start making debt payments and support the new PFAS
  treatment plants when they come online around FY 2027–2028.
  After those projects are finished, he expects debt levels to stabilize, and about five years later the town hopes
  to move forward with the Plant’s Dam project, which could add about 1 million gallons per day of new capacity.
  He acknowledged that residents have already endured several years of steep increases, which is unfortunate
  but necessary to protect public health, meet federal requirements, and keep the water system financially
  stable.

  Note Mr. North outlined several key elements of the proposed water rate structure. First, the plan would create
  separate rate systems for residential and non‑residential customers, allowing each group to be billed more
  fairly. Second, customers who install irrigation submeters would receive a separate, lower irrigation rate,
  though homeowners would still be responsible for the installation cost. A new low‑usage tier would also be
  added for customers using under 10,000 gallons per six months. He noted that although the overall proposal is
  described as a 13% increase, low‑usage customers would see increases closer to about 5%.

   Note He encouraged residents to adopt water‑saving appliances and fixtures, such as low‑flow faucets,
  toilets, and efficient washing machines, explaining that conservation reduces water and sewer demand, lowers
  electricity use, and eases long‑term strain on the system. He also explained that the Department plans to
  simplify the billing structure by removing the old 3,500‑gallon fixed‑fee component, making bills easier for
  customers to read and calculate.

  Note Mr. North also addressed the remaining 50 customers who still have manual‑read meters. Because staff
  must schedule appointments and enter homes to read these meters, the department plans to increase
  manual‑reading fees to encourage replacement of the last outdated units.

   Note He reviewed the proposed FY 2027 water budget, explaining that the chart highlighted the Department’s
  largest expenses, generally items over $100,000. He said the biggest cost drivers are labor, bond interest,
  bond principal payments, and capital projects, and noted that about 40% of the entire water budget is tied to
  existing and future debt. He acknowledged this is a heavy burden, remarking that no one would want 40% of
  their income going to debt, but explained the Department has little choice due to stricter treatment
  requirements, lower contaminant limits, and the need for modern treatment technology.

  Note Mr. North emphasized that this challenge is not unique to East Lyme, as water systems across the
  country are facing tighter regulations, new contaminants, and rising infrastructure costs. North noted that older
  treatment systems may have worked well when built, but regulatory limits keep tightening, and new
  contaminants continue to be added to federal and state rules.

   Note Mr. Bragaw then began the presentation on the proposed sewer rates, noting that although he oversees
  multiple areas including highways and buildings, the Water and Sewer Departments operate as separate
  enterprise funds, fully independent from the Town’s general budget.
  He explained that the sewer system faces different challenges than the water system, including a much smaller
  customer base of approximately 3,700 sewer accounts compared to 7,000 water accounts. East Lyme does
  not have its own treatment plant; instead, all wastewater is pumped 10 miles through New London, and 42
  cents of every dollar collected goes directly to Waterford and New London for treatment costs. The system
  also relies on 23 pump stations, making operations more complex and expensive for a relatively small number
  of ratepayers.

  Note He reviewed the sewer budget, noting that the current budget is $3.1 million, and the proposed FY 2027
  budget is $3.316 million, a 5.6% increase. He explained that although 5.6% sounds like a lot, 96% of the
  increase is driven by four major cost categories, with 76% of the increase tied directly to rising treatment costs
  charged by Waterford and New London. Because East Lyme’s share is based on proportional usage, even
  small changes in flow between towns can shift costs unpredictably. He emphasized that these expenses are
  outside the Town’s control.

   Note Additional cost drivers include health insurance and pension increases, as well as outside services, such
  as funding a new customer portal that will allow residents to check their usage online rather than waiting for a
  six‑month bill. Mr. Bragaw said this tool is important because sewer bills, unlike taxes, can be reduced through
  conservation, but customers need real‑time information to manage their usage.

   Note He also reviewed the sewer system’s capital needs, explaining that much of the infrastructure dates to
  the early 1990s and is aging. The Town must begin setting aside capital funds, something it was unable to do
  until two years ago due to financial constraints.
  Major upcoming needs include upgrades to the Dad’s Restaurant pump station, the Pattagansett pump station,
  and the Bridebrook pump station, as well as work on the 40‑year‑old force main under the Niantic River, which
  requires a bypass system for emergency reliability. Because East Lyme shares downstream infrastructure with
  Waterford and New London, the Town must also contribute to regional capital projects.

   Note Mr. Bragaw then explained how the proposed irrigation submetering changes will affect sewer revenue.
  Historically, customers with irrigation systems were billed sewer charges on all water used, even water applied
  to lawns. Under the new structure, irrigation water will no longer be billed for sewer, reducing sewer revenue
  and requiring adjustments to sewer rates to compensate across the 3,700 customers.

   Note He emphasized that the Water and Sewer Departments are entirely ratepayer‑funded, receive no tax
  revenue, and must remain in full compliance with all public health and environmental regulations. Rising
  chemical and infrastructure costs do not follow the consumer price index, and capital projects often increase by
  10-15% annually.

   Note Mr. Bragaw stated that the proposed sewer usage tiers will now mirror the water tiers for clarity.
  Approximately 30-35% of customers who use under 10,000 gallons per six months will see increases around
  3.5%, while most other customers will see increases around 5%. High‑volume non‑residential users and
  residential customers who irrigate without submeters will see higher increases due to their discretionary usage.

   Note He the public for attending, noting that Water and Sewer hearings typically draw few participants. He
  emphasized the Department’s commitment to transparency, ongoing public updates, and maintaining safe,
  compliant, and reliable water and sewer services. He noted that most customers will not see the impact of the
  new rates until November and reaffirmed the Department’s goal of setting fair and responsible rates while
  managing the realities of operating a complex utility system.

     though East Lyme’s well water already has very low organic content. He explained that PFAS treatment
     does not remove sodium and that sodium treatment is exorbitantly expensive, costing tens of millions of
     dollars. He added that the Town’s wells, including the well north of the highway, are protected under
     wellhead protection regulations, though aquifer‑wide salt contamination is a broader statewide issue.

      Note Ms. Kalal stated that sodium affects not only water‑system customers but also residents on private
     wells drawing from the same aquifer. Mr.Bragaw explained that the Town has been actively working to
     reduce road‑salt usage, including educating staff and contractors on “green snow” practices and
     coordinating with the Board of Education to reduce salt use near two major wells. He noted that public
     safety still requires clear roads, and that the State of Connecticut applies salt on state‑maintained roads
     that run directly through the aquifer, which the Town cannot control.

      Note Ms. Kalal raised concerns about septic‑system impacts on Long Island Sound, citing research by Dr.
     Jamie Vaudrey of UConn. She described the Town of Clinton’s program requiring septic pumping every
     five years, enforced through fines, and suggested East Lyme consider a similar approach. Mr. Bragaw
     responded that staffing and budgeting constraints complicate such programs because water and sewer are
     separate enterprise funds and cannot share Town staff without adding costs to the utility budget.

      Note Ms. Kalal encouraged greater inter‑departmental cooperation to reduce costs and suggested that the
     Conservation of Natural Resources Commission could host public education programs on water
     conservation and safe water practices. Commissioners agreed these were constructive ideas.

      Note She also asked whether the Town should reconsider the pace of new housing development given
     water and sewer constraints. Commissioners explained that decisions about land use and development fall
     under Planning and Zoning, not the Water and Sewer Commission, and that property owners who have
     paid taxes on their land for decades retain the right to develop it under zoning regulations.

     Mr. Zoller seconded the motion.
     Motion carried, 8-0-0.
