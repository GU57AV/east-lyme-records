5. Billing Adjustments/Disputes

  Note see attachment.

   Note Mr. North provided a detailed overview of the leak adjustment request:
  *The property qualifies for Commission review because it is mixed‑use.
  *Staff completed the preliminary review- verified calculations, confirmed the leak was repaired,
  reviewed the repair invoice ($1,400), reviewed appraisal data confirming mixed‑use classification,
  provided photos showing the leak location (underground between the commercial sign and residential units).
  *Normal usage for the property is 24,000 gallons per May–November cycle.
  *During the leak period, usage spiked to 71,000 gallons.
  *The leak was detected after grass appeared unusually green; the radio transmitter had communication
  issues, delaying detection.
  *Notification to the owner occurred on 11/6, and the leak was repaired in mid‑December.

  Note Mr. North reminded everyone that the Commission’s 1‑in‑10 policy requires:
  *At least $1,000 in excess charges.
  *Consideration of whether leaked water entered the sewer system.
  *For mixed‑use or >3‑unit properties, precedent has been not to adjust sewer charges if the leaked water did
  not enter the sewer system.
  *Despite this, staff ran the calculation as if sewer adjustment were allowed, to show the maximum possible
  benefit.

  Note Mr. Roberts asked for clarification on the difference between water and sewer savings.


   Note Mr. North explained the calculation method:
  *The 1‑in‑10 adjustment compares normal usage (24,000 gallons) and leak‑period usage (71,000 gallons).
  *The user pays the midpoint rather than the full high usage.
  *Sewer charges are only adjustable if leaked water entered the sewer system.
  *In this case, it did not, so sewer adjustment is not typically granted.
  *The Commission must still pay New London for sewer flow, which limits flexibility.
  *As a result, the 1‑in‑10 adjustment would reduce the customer’s bill from $1,410 to approximately $740,
  yielding a savings of about $740.

   Note Mr. Bond asked whether the facilities are now fully on the meter and Mr. North confirmed they are. The
  meter had a period during the summer when it stopped communicating. This is not uncommon- meters can be
  hit, moved during construction, or disconnected.
  Because the radio transmitter was not functioning, staff could not detect the leak as early as modern metering
  typically allows. As a result, staff had to rely on the traditional 1‑in‑10 policy, which the property qualifies for
  because the bill was unusually large and the customer had never received a 1‑in‑10 adjustment before.

8. Discussion of Water Budget & Rates for FY27 & Set Public Hearing

  Note see attachment.
         Water_Rates.pdf

  Note Mr. North mentioned how the Subcommittee has met three times and made strong progress on updating
  the rate structure. It was noted that stricter PFAS regulations are coming at both the federal and state levels,
  making it important to stay on schedule with treatment projects. Long-term plans include new treatment plants
  and future water sources.

  Note Mr. North explained that a proposed 12.2% budget increase includes:
  *6.7% for rising operating costs.
  *A large increase to the capital reserve (from about $460,000 to $760,000) to prepare for PFAS-related debt.
  *While there's been a slight decrease in electricity costs due to switching to propane well maintenance costs
  are rising due to aging equipment and water conditions.
  *Health insurance costs are rising, and an additional distribution operator is requested to maintain system
  reliability and balance staffing needs.
  *Staff hold a mix of treatment and distribution licenses, and maintaining flexibility and proper coverage remains
  important.
  *Future debt includes PFAS treatment and new facilities.
  *A potential new water source project is being delayed to avoid sharp rate increases.
  *Water supply is becoming a constraint, and while New London provides some support, it cannot meet long-
  term demand.

  Note *PFAS projects are estimated at $17 million, with significant future debt payments.
  *Rates are expected to increase to cover these costs.
  *The project must be completed by November 2029 to meet regulatory deadlines, and planning is underway
  with a tight schedule.

  Note Ms. Russell asked if the recent big storms put a lot of pressure on operations.


  Note Mr. North said the storms did strain operations. There were few major main breaks, but freeze-thaw
  conditions raise the risk, with breaks typically occurring at the first freeze, the coldest period, and the spring
  thaw.
  This winter also required unusually heavy hydrant clearing. Public Works led the effort with support from the
  Water and Fire departments; many hydrants were buried under 3 to 4 feet of snow, requiring heavy equipment
  and real-time coordination because no single department could handle the volume alone.

   Note It was noted that the Water Department has made major cybersecurity upgrades. They replaced an old
  control system with a newer, more secure system. Support is now better, including secure remote
  troubleshooting. They also plan to apply for state funding that could cover about 80% of additional
  cybersecurity and physical security improvements. Everyone agreed that utilities of all sizes are now targets
  and have to take security seriously.

   Note The Commission continued to talk about how to explain these costs to the public. A key point is that they

   Note Proposed rate changes include separating residential and non-residential customers into different rate
  systems. Right now, everyone is on the same tiered rate.
  Under the proposal:
  *Residential rates would have tiers designed to discourage very high summer use (mostly irrigation).
  *Non-residential customers, including properties with 3+ units, would use a different structure.
  *They prefer using pricing to reduce extreme use because strict enforcement (extra meters, inspections) would
  be difficult and feel intrusive.
  *Their data shows summer water use is very uneven- about 6,300 residential accounts total, about 22% use
  under 10,000 gallons over the summer (and together only use about 4% of the water), and only 92 customers
  use over 80,000 gallons in the summer, but they account for about 26 to 27% of all residential water use; this
  supports the idea that a small number of heavy irrigation users drive most of the summer peak demand.

   Note The Commission discussed how the new rate plan targets lawn watering because very high summer use
  (over 80,000 gallons in six months) is clearly irrigation. Summer production jumps far above winter levels
  mainly due to irrigation. And meeting that peak demand is expensive because the utility has to buy water from
  New London at retail rates and also send a portion of its own water back to New London.

   Note It was discussed how irrigation is a luxury, so heavy irrigators should pay more, and low-use customers
  should not subsidize them. It was explained to the Commission that sewer-based incentives will not work well
  because most of the heaviest irrigators are not in the sewer service area, so the solution has to be built into
  water rates.

   Note The proposal also improves the irrigation sub-meter (deduct meter) option so sewered customers can
  avoid paying sewer charges on irrigation water, shifting revenue to the water system where the costs occur.
  Overall, the goal is fairness, a small number of customers drive a large share of costs, and the new structure
  makes them pay a more proportional share.

   Note Mr. North explained that they're proposing clearer, tiered residential rates. The lowest residential tier (0
  to 10,000 gallons over six months) would only increase about 5% to keep basic indoor water affordable,
  especially for low-use and fixed-income households. Customers who stay under 10,000 gallons should see a
  manageable increase.

   Note They're also proposing a separate non-residential rate, so businesses are not unfairly charged like heavy
  irrigation users. The first business tier would go up to 100,000 gallons to reflect normal higher baseline use,
  while extremely large users (like hospitals or prisons) would fall into higher tiers and pay more.

  Note Mr. Bragaw noted to make bills easier for the public to understand, they're also simplifying terminology
  and calculations. They are renaming the “meter maintenance fee” because customers find the term confusing,
  and the charge reflects real equipment costs like meter and radio replacements, not just routine maintenance.
  They are also removing the old 3,500-gallon offset that made tier billing hard to follow.

  Note The Water and Sewer Departments are working toward launching a customer portal so customers can
  view usage, watch for leaks, and track irrigation patterns without calling the office. This is meant to help people
  manage their consumption under the new rate structure.

9. Discussion of Sewer Budget & Rates for FY27 & Set Public Hearing

  Note see attachment.
       Sewer_Rates.pdf

   Note Mr. Bragaw explained that the sewer budget is increasing by $176,000 (5.6%), and most of that increase
  comes from a small number of items, mainly payments to New London and Waterford, plus smaller amounts
  for a customer portal and sewer assessment collections.

   Note The budget keeps a $400,000 capital reserve, which is important because East Lyme’s reserve has
  dropped to its minimum level over time and New London may soon ask towns to help pay for shared sewer
  infrastructure. The budget is meant to prepare for those future costs. They prefer to pay costs directly instead
  of sending money to New London in advance, because East Lyme can earn better interest by holding its own
  funds.

   Note For the sewer rate structure, they are updating tiers to match the new water tiers, so bills are more
  consistent and fairer. Low-use residential customers (10,000 gallons or less) would see about a 3.5% increase,
  most customers about 5%, and the highest-use customers (80,000+ gallons) about 10%. He added that the
  old sewer tiers were outdated and did not match real usage, and the new approach is meant to support
  conservation and make sure high-use customers pay a more appropriate share.
