To: East Lyme Water & Sewer Commission
Fr: Carol Russell
Re: Master’s Thesis on Community Water Fluoridation by Former Ledge Light Intern, Jessica Seyfried

As some of you may recall, back in October 2013 the Water & Sewer Commission asked me to approach
Ledge Light regarding our on-going dialogue with CT DPH following the publication in October 2012 of a
study on fluoride by researchers (Choi, et al.) with the Harvard School of Public Health. The Harvard
team analyzed 27 studies, mostly conducted in China, regarding fluoride exposure and reduction in IQ.
The Choi study acknowledged insufficiencies within the 27 studies but found “the consistency of their
findings adds support to existing evidence of, ‘fluoride-associated cognitive deficits, and suggests the
potential developmental neurotoxicity of fluoride should be a high research priority.” A follow-up
statement issued by Anna Choi, the lead author of the HSPH study, and Philippe Grandjean, the senior
author, includes the following caution: “These results do not allow us to make any judgment regarding
possible levels of risk at levels of exposure typical for water fluoridation in the U. S. On the other hand,
neither can it be concluded that no risk is present. We therefore recommend further research to clarify
what role fluoride exposure levels may play in possible adverse effects on brain development, so that
future risk assessments can properly take into regard this possible hazard.”

When Ledge Light was made aware of our concerns, they advised one of their interns would be
conducting a thorough search of the available literature regarding water fluoridation. Subsequently,
Jessica Seyfried, a graduate student with the UConn Health Center, contacted me about her project and
later met personally with both me and Brad. Throughout 2014 and into 2015 she conducted her
research — which became her thesis towards completion of a Master’s Degree in Public Health.

Ms. Seyfried’s Master’s Thesis is titled, Revisiting Community Water Fluoridation. The paper explores
the science and the social factors that have influenced (and continue to influence) the formulation of US
policy on the use of CWF to promote dental health. Ms. Seyfried’s analysis identifies weaknesses, based
on today’s standards, within this body of work. Her conclusion cites an urgent need for follow-up
research: “There is a solid argument against the method of CWF delivery now that scientists seem to
agree thot the benefit of fluoride is purely topical......Our nation’s recommendation to expose the
majority of its public to community water ‘fluoridation needs to be based on more sound, replicated
evidence. Until that evidence is produced, the safest way to proceed is to pause community water
fluoridation. During this hiatus, data should continue to be collected on the rates of dental caries, as well
as the occurrence of syndromes or diseases that are hypothesized to potentially be a result of exposure
to systemic fluoride via drinking water. Once this data is analyzed, then a more informed decision can be
made on the best way to prevent our communities from developing dental caries.”

Ms. Seyfried’s thesis devotes an appendix to the question: Is it plausible fluoride is a developmental
neurotoxin? She explains the complexity of this issue and its public health implications. She concludes,
“tt is not clear whether or not fluoride exposure through CWF is a developmental neurotoxin, but what is
clear is that more research on the topic must be done.”

You can access the full thesis document at http://digitalcommons.uconn.edu/gs_theses/861.
