19" Century Funeral Customs

“Many people did not fear death; rather, they feared not being “properly” mourned”
(National Park Service, 2011)

Preparation before the funeral

- It was customary for the clock in the room of the deceased to be stopped at the time
of passing and would not be restarted till after the passing.

- All blinds and curtains were closed

- A black wreath was hung on the doors in order to let others know that someone had
passed (at this time mourning was public and shared with in the community)

- Mirrors and any reflective surfacé Wa Covered in black cloth

- Crepe, a black cheese cloth like material, was draped on mantels, shelves, and
window sills. (White draping would be used if it was a child that had passed)

- Many times herbs such as rosemary were laid out around the parlor to help mask the
smell

- Families would send out funeral cards or invitations to let family and close friends
know of the passing and the day it would be held. These were typically white with
black bordering

- Postmortem postcards’ were sent to family members who were too far away to attend
the funeral.

Body Preparation

After death the body was laid out and washed. Men would wash the deceased if they
were male and women would wash them if they were female. The body was then wrapped in a
shroud that had long sleeves and an open back. It would not be till later on that people would
begin to bury people in their own clothing. Next the body was placed in a coffin in the parlor
usually atop saw horses or two ladder back chairs. After the minister prayed over the body the
coffin was nailed shut and draped in a black shroud to be taken to the cemetery.

Keepsakes

5 - Memorial cards were handed out at the funerals and were considered tokens of the
dead. These had the deceased’s name, birth date, death date, number of years months
and days of life, as well as an uplifting poem or message.
- Mourning portraits were pictures, sketches, or paintings of a family member dressed
» in mourning usually holding a portrait of the dead.
- People would make items that could be used to remember the deceased. Examples are
samplers, hair wreaths, monuments, etc.

Men in Mourning

- Men wore their best dark suit and usually a black band around their arm or hat

- Men were expected to be the strong ones and return to work as soon as the dead were
buried

- Men could remarry shortly after their wife’s death in order to find a mother for his
children

- Depending on how long ago the wife had died the new wife might have to take up
mourning for the deceased

Changing Times

- By the 1880’s hospitals had become an acceptable place to die
- Funeral director had become a profession and funerals were occurring in funeral
parlors

- Grief became less public

- Coffins changed shape and became caskets by the late 19" century

- People were no longer being buried as much on personal property

- The first rural cemetery was established in Boston, Mount Auburn Cemetery
S - Tombstones became more elaborate

Explore History!
Smith-Harris House
Monday, October 12th
12-4 pm, Free

Looking for something fun to do on Columbus Day? Join the Smith-Harris House as we host
family-friendly activities that explore history! Try your hand at painting a “mural,” make a
hankie doll, and test your skill at “squash bowling,” “graces,” “hoop roll,” and other historic
games played by children two centuries ago! Take a tour to discover what life in an East Lyme
farmhouse was like in the 1800s! Visit our website for more information at www.smithharris.org
or contact director Joanie DiMartino at 860-739-0761 or curator@smithharris.org.

Spirit Voices: Victorian Mourning and Spiritualism
Smith-Harris House & the Old Stone Church Burial Ground
Saturday, October 24th

3-6 pm.

Open-House is free

Tours of the Burial Ground: $10 per person/$30 per family
Tour times: 3:30, 4:30, 5:30

Discover Victorian-Era rituals of death and dying at the Smith-Harris House! From 19th-century
funeral traditions such as “widow’s weeds” and “safety coffins,” to the rise of the séance, learn
how those who died were remembered and honored by loved ones. Then, take a 45-minute tour
of the Old Burial Ground and encounter former residents of the Smith-Harris House, as they
share vignettes of their former lives! To purchase tour tickets, contact director Joanie DiMartino
at 860-739-0761 or curator@smithharris.org. For more information, visit our website at
www.smithharris.org.
